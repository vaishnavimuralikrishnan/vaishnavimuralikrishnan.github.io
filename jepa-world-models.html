What a model has to throw away to understand physics

I trained two small models to predict the next frame of a two-body elastic collision — one that predicts pixels directly, and one that predicts in a compressed latent space (JEPA). The pixel model got a loss three orders of magnitude lower. It had also, I think, learned almost nothing about the physics.

Task — 64×64 two-ball elastic collision, no gravity Models — SimpleJEPA (1.9M params) vs. Pixel Transformer (2.7M params) Tools — pymunk · PyTorch
01

The setup

I wanted a physical system small enough to fully simulate and inspect, but with real dynamics — momentum transfer, elastic collisions, wall bounces — so that "understanding the physics" would mean something concrete rather than just curve-fitting. pymunk gives two balls, no gravity, perfectly elastic collisions in a bounded 64×64 box, rendered to raw RGB frames — 10,000 of them, one simulation step apart.

The interesting question wasn't whether a neural network could predict a bouncing ball. It was what representation it would need to build in order to do it well over many steps, not just one.

02

Two ways to predict a frame

Both models take frame t and predict frame t+1. They differ entirely in where the prediction happens.

Pixel Transformer — predicts in pixel space

Splits each 64×64 frame into 64 patches of 8×8 pixels, embeds them, runs six Transformer-encoder layers with learned positional embeddings, and projects straight back out to pixel patches. There is no bottleneck between input and output beyond the embedding dimension — every patch keeps talking to every other patch, at full spatial resolution, the whole way through. 

class PixelTransformer(nn.Module):
    # patchify -> +pos_embed -> 6x TransformerEncoderLayer -> unpatchify
    # embed_dim=192, heads=8, layers=6 — 2,718,528 params

JEPA — predicts in latent space

Encodes the frame down through four conv layers into a single 64-dimensional vector — a bottleneck 96× narrower than the 12,288 raw pixel values it started from. A small MLP predictor steps that vector forward in time; a decoder is only used afterward, to check the prediction against pixels for the loss. 

class SimpleJEPA(nn.Module):
    # encoder: 64x64x3 --[4 conv layers]--> z (64-dim)
    # predictor: z_t --[MLP]--> z_t+1
    # decoder: z --[4 deconv layers]--> 64x64x3   (for loss + inspection only)
    # 1,925,059 params
the actual difference

The Transformer never has to compress anything — it can pass spatial detail straight through. The JEPA is forced, every single step, to squeeze the entire scene through a 64-number needle. That constraint turns out to matter more than either architecture's capacity.

03

Training loss looked like a verdict

where this reading goes wrong

One-step MSE this low, on a deterministic simulation with a fixed frame ordering, is a red flag more than a result. It's the signature of a model that has effectively indexed the trajectory rather than learned the rule that generates it — and the rollout below is what exposes that.

04

Open-loop rollout: feeding a model its own guesses

Training loss only ever asks a model to predict one step ahead from real context. To see what a model actually understands, I fed each model's own prediction back in as the next input, for 15 steps, with no ground truth in the loop — an open-loop rollout.

what the flat orange line actually means

A flat, near-zero line for 15 steps of self-fed rollout is not stability — on this task it's a tell. The Transformer isn't tracking momentum and elastic collisions step by step; it's reproducing a trajectory it already has memorized, patch by patch, regardless of what "should" happen physically next. The JEPA's error is higher and drifts upward, but it's an honest number: it reflects a model actually integrating a dynamics rule forward, accumulating the small errors that come with that, the way any approximate physical model does.

05

The teleport test

The clearest way to tell "memorized trajectory" from "manipulable state" is to reach in and change the state mid-rollout, then see whether the model can keep going from the new situation. Five steps of ordinary rollout, then an intervention on the latent vector — a forced jump in position — then five more steps, unassisted.

what I'm not claiming

Editing a latent vector and getting a coherent continuation is a controlled observation on one small, deterministic, two-body system — not evidence of general causal reasoning. I also didn't identify a clean "x-position dimension" the way find_x_dim.py was written to try to do; the intervention here is a uniform bump across all 64 dimensions, which the model apparently still resolves into a sensible new state. That's interesting, but it's a weaker and more specific claim than "the model has disentangled physical variables."

06

What I actually take from this

The headline loss number was the least informative statistic in the whole experiment. The Transformer's 0.000003 wasn't a better model of the physics — it was a model with no incentive to build one, because nothing in its architecture forced it to give anything up. The JEPA's worse loss was the cost of a bottleneck doing its job: 64 numbers can't encode 12,288 pixels' worth of arbitrary detail, so the only frames it can predict well are ones that follow a rule.

That's the part that felt like physics to me, more than machine learning — a bottleneck is a modeling choice with consequences, the same way choosing which quantities to track (position and momentum, say, and not the exact pixel-level rendering of a ball) is a modeling choice with consequences. Throwing information away on purpose, in the right place, is what left this model with something to intervene on at all.
